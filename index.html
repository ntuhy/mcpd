Pennsylvania v. Mimms (1977)
During a lawful traffic stop for an expired license plate, officers ordered the driver out of the vehicle. A bulge in his jacket led to a pat-down that recovered a handgun. The Supreme Court held that ordering a driver out of a car during a lawful stop is a minimal intrusion justified by officer safety and does not violate the Fourth Amendment. A subsequent frisk based on reasonable suspicion of a weapon is also permissible.

Terry v. Ohio (1968)
Police may briefly stop a person based on reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot and may frisk the outer clothing for weapons if they reasonably believe the person is armed and dangerous. This “Terry stop” requires less than probable cause and is justified by officer safety. The limited search must stay within the scope needed to detect weapons.

United States v. Dunn (1987)
The Supreme Court held that a barn located about 60 yards from a house on a large ranch was not within the curtilage of the home and thus fell under the open-fields doctrine. It established a four-factor test to determine curtilage: (1) proximity to the home, (2) whether the area is within an enclosure surrounding the home, (3) the nature of the uses to which the area is put, and (4) the steps taken to protect the area from observation. Observations of the barn from open fields did not require a warrant.

Quick Review - Full Opinion
Maryland v. Wilson (1997)
Extending Mimms, the Court ruled that police may order passengers (not just the driver) out of a vehicle during a lawful traffic stop. The decision rests on the same officer-safety rationale: the presence of passengers increases potential danger, while the additional intrusion on the passenger is minimal. Evidence discovered as a result (here, cocaine that fell as the passenger exited) may be admitted.

Quick Review - Full Opinion
Graham v. Connor (1989)
Claims that law enforcement used excessive force during an arrest, investigatory stop, or other seizure of a free citizen are analyzed under the Fourth Amendment’s objective reasonableness standard, not under substantive due process. The question is whether the force was objectively reasonable from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene, considering the totality of the circumstances (including severity of the crime, threat posed, and resistance).

Quick Review - Full Opinion
California v. Greenwood (1988)
The Supreme Court ruled that the Fourth Amendment does not prohibit the warrantless search and seizure of garbage left for collection outside the curtilage of a home. There is no reasonable expectation of privacy in trash placed at the curb because it is readily accessible to the public, animals, scavengers, and trash collectors. Evidence found in such garbage may be used to obtain a search warrant for the home.

Quick Review - Full Opinion
Miranda v. Arizona (1966)
The Court held that statements obtained during custodial interrogation are inadmissible unless the suspect is first informed of the right to remain silent, that anything said can be used against them, the right to an attorney, and the right to appointed counsel if indigent. These procedural safeguards are required to protect the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.

Quick Review - Full Opinion
Carroll v. United States (1925)
Established the automobile exception to the Fourth Amendment warrant requirement. Police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe it contains evidence of a crime or contraband, because of the vehicle’s inherent mobility and the practical difficulty of obtaining a warrant before it can be moved.

Full Opinion
Warden v. Hayden (1967)
The Supreme Court abolished the old “mere evidence” rule. Police may seize items that have only evidentiary value (not just fruits, instrumentalities, or contraband) during a lawful search. The Court also upheld a warrantless entry and search of a home under the hot-pursuit/exigent-circumstances exception when officers were chasing an armed robbery suspect who had just entered the residence.
